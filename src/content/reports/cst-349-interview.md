### Introduction

I started by clicking-through all the LinkedIn accounts in the "CS Online Student
Profiles, Groups and Learning Journal - All Cohorts" sheet, making sure to message
every working link. I must have sent over 20+ linkedin message requests. Almost all
never answered, however, one person from Cohort 9 did. After going back and forth for
a while in LinkedIn chat, they did agree to answer my question but requested to keep
their name and company private: "I'd prefer to keep my name and company anonymous
since some of my work involves products and projects that aren't appropriate to
discuss publicly. You can describe me generally as a Software QA Engineer working on
AI-powered consumer products."

They weren't available to hop on a video or phone call, so I sent all my questions
over and they answered on their own time. The overlap turned out to be coincidence,
since I work in QA myself and have been testing AI output at my job for months.

### Summary

The key takeaway that mattered most was related to testing non-deterministic
software. Traditional software lets you say, in their words, "Given this input, I
expect this exact output," and an AI product does not, because several responses to
the same question can all be acceptable. So you also have to judge whether the
response was accurate, relevant, helpful, and appropriate, which they said "makes
defining 'pass' and 'fail' much more complicated."

They didn't initially plan to be in this field. They started as Sales and Technical
specialists within the company and studying computer science pulled them toward
automation until they got an opportunity in QA engineering. They do not treat QA and
development as separate tracks, since this job lets them do both.

With AI advancement, they expect QA to get more technical rather than less. Repetitive
testing will be automated, which leaves QA engineers with test strategy, automation,
and risk. On AI products they also expect QA to help decide what counts as good in the
first place, moving from whether a feature works to whether the output is useful,
reliable, and consistent. Their own workflow shifted the same way, since AI now helps
them read unfamiliar code and dig into failures. Their view is that it moves where the
engineer spends the day rather than replacing them.

Their advice came in two parts. The first was that "you don't need to know everything
before taking on a technical challenge," because the skill that lasts is breaking a
problem down and asking good questions. The second was about communication. Finding a
bug is the easy half, and explaining what happened and why it matters is what makes
the finding worth anything. I very much can relate to this sentiment: bugs are
relatively easy to find as long as you have a solid test plan with defined acceptance
criterias. The hard part is relaying the issue, and providing detailed steps on how to
reproduce the issue in a controlled environment.

### Reflection

It was nice finding someone at another company who is going through a similar
experience. I had been treating AI output testing as a local problem, and a QA
engineer on a much larger product turns out to be facing the same issues. The
interview also changed what I think about the job market. I recently had a lot of
pessimism about QA being automated away and half expected the industry to shift and be
phased out but my perspective has changed. This person has been hired 6 months ago so
there must still be a demand for our line of work.

### Future Steps

I plan to stay in QA, and this conversation made me more comfortable with that
decision. There were a lot of interesting talking points but I honestly don't have a
lot of key takeaways to apply towards my professional journey currently. The world of
AI is constantly changing and the insight I learn today might be irrelevant next year
so the best I can really do is to adapt.
